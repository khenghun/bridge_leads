# Play Solver v1.3 — Expert opponents

A toggle that makes the solver **infer from the opponents' plays**, not only
from which cards they played. Status: **design agreed, 2026-09-01**. First
drafted the same day (opponents judged double-dummy), revised after review to
judge opponents **by what they could see** (a recursive Monte-Carlo), and
revised again the same evening with a measured cost model and a two-stage
algorithm that keeps the recursive judgement while cutting its cost by
roughly an order of magnitude. The user's decisions on the three open
choices are recorded at the end: fast-accept by default with a strict toggle,
hard rejection, and a tolerance that scales out of the sampling noise.

## The gap it closes

v1.0 samples the two hidden hands consistent with the play so far in the
weakest sense: a hidden seat holds the cards it has played, and a seat that
showed out is capped in that suit. Nothing else about *how* they played
enters. So at trick 8, when East followed low to a diamond at trick 3 instead
of returning one for West to ruff, the sampler still deals East the last
diamond as often as the card counts allow — and grades declarer's play on
layouts a decent player would have ruled out.

Under **Expert opponents** a sampled layout must also satisfy: *on this
layout, every earlier play by an opponent was a best play **given what that
opponent could see at the time**.* The layout where East held the last
diamond and a ruff was available is rejected, because on it East — looking at
East's own hand, dummy and the play so far — would have found the ruff. The
surviving sample is the one a strong player reasons with, and the graded best
card moves with it: the second-order inference ("they didn't, so they can't")
that no free tool does. (Bethe's thesis calls exactly this the missing
*negative inference* in Monte-Carlo bridge play, with a ♠Q-placement example
on which both GIB and his own MCTS went down — see *Sources*.)

## Definition — a Monte-Carlo inside the Monte-Carlo

For a graded decision at play index `i`, viewed by seat `V` with opponents
`O` (both defenders when `V` is declarer; declarer alone when `V` is a
defender — partner is *not* judged, see below):

1. **Outer sample.** Draw a layout `L` exactly as v1.0 does (pinned cards,
   show-outs, the user's per-seat constraints).
2. **Judge each earlier opponent decision.** For every index `j < i` where
   the player on play was some `o ∈ O` with a real choice:
   - `o`'s information at `j` is: `o`'s own hand **as dealt in `L`**, dummy
     (real, faced after the opening lead), the play `play[:j]`, and the
     auction — so the same per-seat constraints the user entered, because the
     auction was public to `o` too.
   - **Inner sample.** Draw `M` layouts of the two hands hidden from `o`,
     consistent with that information (the ordinary v1.0 sampler, viewed
     from `o`). DD-solve the position after `play[:j]` on each; one solve
     prices every legal card of `o` on that deal. **Each card's value is its
     mean over the inner deals** — the single-dummy valuation, exactly as
     the grader values the graded seat's own cards. Not the per-deal
     `best − actual` averaged: that is double-dummy thinking (strategy
     fusion), under which a 50-50 guess costs half a trick whichever way it
     is taken and would always be "inconsistent". Compared card by card over
     the same deals, a coin-flip guess is a tie.
   - `o`'s actual card at `j` is **consistent** unless some alternative card
     is **shown** to be better by more than the tolerance — *shown* meaning
     the paired difference over the common deals clears the tolerance by a
     confidence margin (see *Tolerance and noise*). Otherwise `L` is
     discarded.
3. Keep drawing outer layouts until `num_deals` consistent ones are found or
   the draw budget is spent; grade `i` on the survivors.

This is one level of recursion: the inner sample judges `o` against
opponents who are merely card-consistent, not themselves expert. That is the
same shape as Bouzy et al.'s level-one recursive Monte-Carlo (a PIMC player
used as the simulator of a PIMC player), which is the level at which they
measured the gain (+0.5 trick per deal over plain PIMC at 13 cards) and past
which the gain stopped adding up. Depth stays a parameter (`depth = 1`);
depth 2 is the same code at the inner count squared — a knob, not a plan.

Only plays before `i` count, including opponents' cards in the current
partial trick. A tolerance is necessary, not optional: the inner mean is a
noisy estimate from `M` deals, and "best within noise" is the honest reading
of "found the best play". It is also what keeps the model from being brittle:
Buro et al. note that a deterministic simulator makes `P(move | world)` either
0 or 1, which "makes the prediction brittle in the face of players who do not
play identically to ourselves" — the tolerance is our softening.

### Partner is deliberately not judged

For a defender's grade, partner's cards are **signals to you** — a low card
in UDCA encouraging, a high one discouraging, count and suit preference — and
judging them as pure trick-maximising play would reject honest signalling
layouts. Partner stays pinned-but-unjudged as in v1.0. Reading the signals
themselves is a separate, far-later feature (see the roadmap's *Later* list:
*Partnership signalling*).

## Cost, measured

Everything below rests on one fact about DDS that the first draft did not
have: **a solve's cost falls off a cliff as the hand progresses.** Measured
on the dev laptop (4 DDS threads, `solve_all_boards` batches of 40, random
legal play prefixes, NT and a suit contract alternating):

| Cards played | 0 | 4 | 8 | 12 | 16 | 20 | 24+ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ms / board (all cards priced) | 7.7 | 3.5 | 1.8 | 1.2 | 0.37 | 0.20 | ≤ 0.15 |

So a trick-1 solve costs ~50× a trick-7 solve. The prod box (2 vCPU, DDS
capped at 2 threads) is roughly 3–4× slower per board; real positions are
somewhat harder than random-play ones. Two more measurements matter:

- **`analyse_all_plays`** (DDS's `AnalyseAllPlaysBin`, batches of 20) prices
  a whole **48-card play sequence on one layout in 6.3 ms** — the DD value
  after every card, for less than one trick-1 solve, because it reuses the
  transposition table down the line. `analyse_play` one at a time is 16 ms.
- **`SolveMode.OptimalOne`** (one best card, no full pricing) is ~2× cheaper
  than the default all-cards solve (0.8 vs 1.6 ms at 8 played). It does not
  help here: the inner verdict needs `best` *and* the actual card's value,
  and one default solve gives both.

### The naive cost

One graded decision costs `N` boards today. Judging every earlier opponent
decision by a fresh inner sample costs `N × overhead × k_i × M` boards, where
`k_i` is the number of earlier opponent decisions with a choice (roughly
`i / 2`) and `M = ratio × N`. At `N = 30`, `M = 15`, mid-hand `k_i = 10`,
overhead 1.5: ~6,700 boards per decision, and — the part the first draft
missed — those boards are at *earlier* positions than the decision, so they
cost more each: ≈ 2.5 ms average, ~17 s per decision on the laptop, **~4 min
per seat, ~15 min on prod.** Plus a sampler build (10–30 ms) per inner
sample. Bouzy et al. report the same order: their level-one recursion is
"one order of magnitude" slower per level, 5 hours per deal at their sizes.

### What the algorithm below does about it

1. **A double-dummy trace as a fast-accept, not as the judge.** One
   `analyse_all_plays` call per outer layout gives the DD cost of *every*
   earlier opponent card on that layout at once. **If the actual card cost
   nothing double-dummy on `L`, it is accepted as consistent without an
   inner sample** — an expert could not have done better on this layout than
   the card they found. Only cards that *lost a DD trick on `L`* are
   **suspects**, and only suspects get the recursive single-dummy judgement
   that decides whether the loss was a reasonable single-dummy play (kept)
   or a play an expert would not have made (layout rejected). Nothing is
   ever rejected without the recursive judgement; the shortcut only
   *accepts*. This is the one deliberate deviation from the pure definition
   (see *Strict mode* under the open choices) and it removes most of the
   cost, because on most layouts most opponent plays lose nothing.
2. **Memoise the judgement on `(j, o, o's holding at j)`** — dummy,
   `play[:j]` and the constraints are the same for every outer layout, so the
   verdict is a function of that key alone. The memo lives in a process-level
   LRU keyed additionally on the deal and the settings, so it survives across
   the chunked requests of one analysis and across the whole-table flow's
   per-seat requests. Late in the hand holdings repeat constantly (few
   cards, many pins); early they rarely do — but early `j` is also where
   suspects are rare, since a card that loses a DD trick at trick 2 is a
   genuine error more often than a guess. Seed the inner rng from the key so
   a verdict is a deterministic function of its key (reproducible, and safe
   to cache).
3. **Sequential inner sampling.** Draw the inner sample in batches of 5 up
   to `M`, and stop as soon as the verdict is settled by the rule in
   *Tolerance and noise*: some alternative's lower confidence bound clears
   the tolerance → inconsistent; every alternative's upper bound is under it
   → consistent; otherwise continue, and at `M` an undecided judgement is
   **consistent** (rejection needs evidence). Clear-cut cases (the great
   majority) settle in the first batch; only borderline plays pay for the
   full `M`. A racing/early-stopping rule of this kind is the standard way to
   spend Monte-Carlo effort where it matters.
4. **Equivalent cards are not a decision.** Cards of one suit that are
   adjacent in rank once the played cards are removed (♠73 from `KJ973`,
   ♣64 from `J64`) are one choice; a seat whose legal cards form a single
   class made no decision at `j`, so there is nothing to judge. (Same
   grouping Buro et al. use for move pruning; the lead app already merges
   such leads in its table.) Cuts `k_i` by a third or more.
5. **Most-recent-first, early exit.** A layout's suspects are judged from
   the latest `j` backwards; the first inconsistent one rejects the layout.
   Recent decisions prune most, so a rejected layout usually pays for one
   judgement, not `k_i`.
6. **Rounds, and big batches.** The outer loop works on a pool of layouts at
   a time: trace them all (one `analyse_all_plays` batch per 20), then in
   each round take the next suspect of every still-pending layout, pack all
   their inner boards (memo misses only) into ≤ 200-board `solve_all`
   batches, update verdicts, accept/reject. DDS stays multithreaded, the
   lock is taken a few times per round rather than per judgement, and boards
   sharing `(j, o)` sit together in a batch, where DDS's own transposition
   reuse ("intelligent batching") helps.
7. **Stop when `N` are accepted.** Pending layouts are abandoned unjudged;
   acceptance is a function of the layout alone, so stopping early does not
   bias the survivors.

### Estimated cost after that

Per graded decision at `N = 30`, pool ≈ 2·N (most layouts pass stage 1),
laptop figures; prod ≈ ×3–4:

| Stage | Work | Time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Outer sampling | 60 layouts, one sampler build | ~0.05 s |
| DD trace | 60 × `analyse_all_plays` (≤ 6 ms each, shorter early) | ~0.3 s |
| Inner judgements | ~1.5 suspects per layout before accept/reject × ~8 boards (sequential) × ~2 ms, minus memo hits; plus a sampler build per memo miss | ~1–2 s |
| Grading `i` | 30 boards, as today | ~0.05 s |

**≈ 2 s per decision, ≈ 30 s per seat on the laptop, 1.5–2 min on prod** —
against ~4 / ~15 min naive: roughly 5–10× cheaper, and slow enough that the
progressive (per-trick) requests below are still needed for a usable UI. The
suspect fraction and memo hit rate are the two numbers this estimate hangs
on; the first implementation task measures them on the six example hands
before the defaults are fixed.

## The algorithm

```
grade_decision_expert(P_i, V, settings):
    J  ← opponent decisions before i: (j, o, c_j) for j < i, o ∈ O(V),
         o's legal cards at j forming ≥ 2 equivalence classes
    accepted ← []; drawn ← 0
    while |accepted| < N and drawn < budget·N:
        pool ← sample_layouts(P_i, view=V, count=min(2N, budget·N − drawn))   # v1.0 sampler
        drawn += |pool|
        traces ← analyse_all_plays(pool, play[:i])                              # one DD value per card, per layout
        for L in pool:
            suspects(L) ← [ (j, o, c_j) ∈ J : dd_cost(L, j) > 0 ]   # cost in o's side's tricks
                           ordered by j descending
            if strict: suspects(L) ← J (all of them, latest first)
        pending ← pool
        while pending and |accepted| < N:
            round ← [ (L, next suspect of L) for L in pending ]
            for (L, (j, o, c_j)) in round:
                key ← (deal, j, o, holding(L, o, j), constraints, settings)
                if key ∉ memo: schedule inner sample for key
            run scheduled inner samples: batches of 5 boards per key, ≤ 200 per solve_all,
                per key keep the per-card trick totals over the common deals;
                sequential stop (some alternative's LCB > tol / all alternatives' UCB < tol / M reached → consistent);
                memo[key] ← (consistent?, best_gap, n)
            for (L, s) in round:
                if not memo[key(L, s)].consistent: reject L
                elif L has no more suspects: accept L
        # layouts still pending when |accepted| = N are dropped unjudged
    if accepted is empty: grade on the unfiltered pool, mark expert_inference = 'none'
    else grade i on accepted (the v1.0 _solve), report sampled=drawn, consistent=|accepted|,
         judged = memo misses run, traced = |pool| total
```

`dd_cost(L, j)` reads the trace: the DD value (declarer tricks) after
`play[:j]` versus after `play[:j+1]`, signed so that a loss for `o`'s side is
positive. The inner judgement reads, from a default-mode solve at the
position after `play[:j]` on each inner deal (where `o` is on play), the
tricks for `o`'s side of every legal card, and keeps running per-card totals;
the verdict compares card means, never per-deal maxima. Two perspective
frames, deliberately in two functions (see *Engine changes*).

## Tolerance and noise

The question "was the actual card within `tolerance` of the best card?" is
asked of a noisy estimate, so the tolerance has to sit above the noise or the
filter rejects honest plays at random — and the noise shrinks with the inner
sample, so a fixed number is wrong at every `M` but one. The user's ruling:
**the threshold scales with the sample size so that it is out of the noise.**

The statistic is the **paired difference** `D_c = tricks(c) − tricks(actual)`
per inner deal, for each alternative card `c`, over the *same* deals (one
solve prices every card, so the pairing is free and removes the deal-to-deal
variance from the comparison). `D_c` is 0 on deals where the two cards come
to the same thing and ±1 (rarely ±2) where they differ; for two genuinely
competing plays it differs on perhaps 30–60% of deals, so its standard
deviation is about **0.6 tricks** — the planning figure below. It is
estimated live from the sample, with a floor of 0.3 so a tiny all-alike
sample cannot claim certainty.

The rule, with `m` deals drawn so far, `mean_c` and `se_c = sd_c / √m`:

- **Reject** the layout if for some alternative `mean_c − z·se_c > tol₀`
  — `c` is shown to be better than the actual card by more than the margin.
- **Accept** (stop early) if for every alternative `mean_c + z·se_c < tol₀`.
- Otherwise draw the next batch; at the cap `M`, undecided → **accept**.

`tol₀` is the *substantive* margin — how much better an alternative must be
before an expert would be expected to find it — and `z` the confidence. The
effective rejection threshold is then `tol₀ + z·σ/√M`, which is what scales:

| Outer `N` | Inner cap `M` (ratio 0.5, floor 8) | `se` at σ = 0.6 | Reject only if the gap exceeds ≈ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 10 | 8 | 0.21 | 0.52 |
| 20 | 10 | 0.19 | 0.48 |
| 30 | 15 | 0.15 | 0.41 |
| 50 | 25 | 0.12 | 0.34 |
| **60 (default)** | **30** | **0.11** | **0.32** |
| 100 | 50 | 0.08 | 0.27 |
| 200 | 100 | 0.06 | 0.22 |

with **`tol₀ = 0.10` tricks and `z = 2.0`**. Why those: a tenth of a trick is
below what any player distinguishes at the table, so it is a floor that
never rejects a real tie; and `z = 2` is a one-sided 97.7% bound against the
null "the alternative is better by exactly `tol₀`" — against the common null
"they are equal" it is a 2.7σ event at `M = 15`, a false rejection about one
judgement in 300. Sequential peeking (batches of 5) inflates that a little;
it is still far below the rate at which real opponents err. At the default
`M = 15` the filter therefore rejects a play only when it is shown ~0.4
tricks worse than an alternative — a missed ruff or a failed unblock, not a
matter of taste — and at `M = 50` it tightens to ~0.27. The user raises `N`
to sharpen the filter, and the threshold follows.

The two knobs exposed are `expert_tolerance` (`tol₀`, default 0.10) and, in
the advanced panel, `expert_confidence` (`z`, default 2.0); `M` follows `N`
through the ratio. The response reports, per decision, the effective
threshold actually applied (`tol₀ + z·σ̂/√M`) so the UI can say "rejects
plays shown ≥ 0.32 tricks worse" (at the default 60 deals).

## Configuration — ratios, all exposed

| Setting | Default | Range | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `expert_opponents` | off | bool | the toggle |
| `num_deals` | 60 when on (UI swaps the default from 40) | 5–200 | outer sample size `N` |
| `expert_inner_ratio` | 0.5 | 0.1–1.0 | inner sample cap `M = max(8, round(ratio × N))` — the user's "half of the main Monte-Carlo"; the sequential rule usually stops well short of it |
| `expert_tolerance` | 0.10 tricks | 0–1 | `tol₀`, the substantive margin an alternative must beat the actual card by; the noise allowance `z·σ̂/√M` is added on top (see *Tolerance and noise*) |
| `expert_confidence` | 2.0 | 1–4 | `z`, how many standard errors of evidence a rejection needs (advanced) |
| `expert_budget` | 20 | 5–50 | outer draws allowed per consistent layout wanted, before giving up |
| `expert_strict` | off | bool | judge *every* opponent decision by the inner sample, no double-dummy fast-accept (the pure definition; several times slower) — needed to catch a play that worked on the actual layout but was wrong single-dummy, e.g. an anti-percentage drop that happened to find the queen |
| `expert_depth` | 1 | 1–2 | recursion depth (2 is exposed, not recommended) |

Defaults live in one place in the engine (`engine/play/expert.py`), the API
schema mirrors them, and the UI shows the toggle plus an *Advanced* disclosure
for the ratio, tolerance and strict mode. Everything is in the request body,
so the cache key and the query log carry the settings for free.

## Progressive requests (do this first — it helps v1.1 too)

`AnalyzeRequest` gains an optional `decisions: list[int]` (play indices to
grade); the frontend chunks a seat's analysis **by trick** so the table fills
in as each lands instead of after one long wait. Each chunk is separately
cached; the judgement memo is process-level, so chunking costs no memo hits.
With the cost above this is what makes the mode usable on prod at all.

## What the response says

Every graded decision carries `sampled` (outer draws), `consistent`
(layouts graded), `traced` (layouts that went through the DD trace) and
`judged` (inner judgements actually run — memo hits excluded) when the
toggle is on; the UI shows "graded on 27 of 30 layouts consistent with
expert play". If **zero** layouts survive the budget, the decision is graded
on the unfiltered sample and marked `expert_inference: 'none'` with the note
"no sampled layout is consistent with the opponents' earlier plays — they
may have erred". That is information, not a failure: it says an opponent made
a real mistake earlier, which is worth surfacing in its own right (and with
v1.1's whole-table view, that mistake is probably already in the list).

## What is still approximate

- **Depth 1.** The opponent is modelled as a good single-dummy player whose
  own opponents are merely consistent. Real experts also read *your* plays.
  Deeper recursion is the same code at prohibitive cost, and Bouzy et al.
  found the second recursion did not add up even at 5 cards a hand.
- **The fast-accept is lenient.** A card that happens to lose nothing on
  layout `L` is accepted even if, single-dummy, it was a poor percentage
  play — so the filter keeps some layouts the pure definition would reject.
  It never rejects one the pure definition would keep. `expert_strict`
  removes the shortcut at several times the cost.
- **Noise.** `M` deals give a noisy valuation; the confidence bound absorbs
  it, at the price of power: at the default `M = 15` a play has to be shown
  ~0.4 tricks worse to be rejected, so a genuinely inferior play that is only
  0.2 worse is kept. Raising `N` tightens that.
- **Uniform prior.** Consistent layouts are kept with weight 1; a layout on
  which the opponent's play was *barely* best counts the same as one where it
  was clearly best. The user chose hard rejection; soft weighting stays a
  possible later refinement on the same data.
- **Signals ignored.** Partner's spot cards carry information the model does
  not read (see above).

## Engine changes

- New `engine/play/expert.py`: the settings dataclass with defaults; the
  opponent-decision index (`(j, o, actual_card)` for every `j` with ≥ 2
  equivalence classes, computed **once per graded seat** and sliced per
  `i`); `dd_costs(layout, play, i)` over an `analyse_all_plays` trace;
  the inner judgement (`judge(key) → verdict`, sequential, memoised); the
  outer pool/round loop with budget, early stop and the zero-survivor
  fallback.
- `engine/dds_runtime.py` gains `analyse_plays(deals, plays)` — the
  **third** batch entry point, `AnalyseAllPlays` caps at **20** boards per
  call (not 200), same lock, same per-board fallback to `analyse_play`.
  Still the only module that touches DDS.
- `engine/play/grader.py`: `_sample_layouts(..., expert=None)` delegates to
  the expert loop when settings are given; `grade_play` / `grade_position`
  accept `expert` and `decisions`, and add `sampled` / `consistent` /
  `traced` / `judged` per decision. The inner sample reuses
  `infer_constraints` + `generate_layouts` viewed from `o` — the same
  functions, a different view seat; that reuse is the point of the
  view-parameterised design.
- `app/common/cache.py`: a second, longer-lived `ResultCache` instance for
  judgement verdicts (small values, high count; TTL an hour, a few thousand
  entries).
- **Perspective trap, three times.** `solve_board` returns tricks for the
  side on play; `analyse_play` returns declarer tricks after each card (check
  `declarer_is_first` semantics against a known deal before trusting it);
  and `o`'s "best" is best **for `o`'s side**, the opposite side from the
  graded seat. Keep the frames in separate, individually tested functions.

## Measured (2026-09-01, laptop, `scripts/measure_expert.py --deals 20`)

Six example hands, every gradeable seat, fast-accept mode, N = 20:

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Wall-clock | 164 s over 205 decisions — **0.8 s per decision**, 1–20 s per seat (prod ≈ ×3–4) |
| Layouts kept | 61% of the layouts examined (per seat 20%–99%) |
| Judgements | 1,946 run, 2,513 answered from the memo — **56% hit rate** |
| σ̂ (paired difference) | **mean 0.62, median 0.55** — the planning figure of 0.6 stands, the threshold table is unchanged |
| Grades changed | 18 of 205 |
| Zero-survivor decisions | 0 |

Where the time goes is not where the plan expected: the recursive judgements
are cheap (~10 ms each after the memo), and the **double-dummy trace is
~20 ms per layout on real lines** (three times the 6 ms random-play lines
gave) and dominates. So the outer loop draws only the deficit each round —
every traced layout is examined — rather than a 2N pool. Strict mode on
Board 17 declarer: 109 s against 15 s (4,223 memo entries against 258).

## Implementation order

1. **Measure before tuning.** A script over the six example hands: for each
   seat and decision, the suspect fraction (share of `(L, j)` pairs with DD
   cost > 0), the memo hit rate by trick, and wall-clock per decision at
   `N = 30` — with and without the fast-accept. These fix the defaults and
   confirm (or correct) the estimate above; if the suspect fraction is much
   above ~30%, sequential stopping and the memo carry more of the load than
   planned and the budget default moves.
2. Progressive `decisions` chunking (backend + frontend), on its own — it
   ships value for v1.1 even before the toggle exists.
3. `dds_runtime.analyse_plays`, `expert.py` with the trace, the index, the
   memoised sequential judge, the pool/round loop.
4. Grader integration, schema, service, cache.
5. UI: toggle, advanced panel, per-decision counts, changelog entry.

## Tests that carry the feature

- **Constructed end-position, brute force.** A small position (e.g. 4 cards
  each) where an opponent's earlier play is single-dummy best only on
  layouts where they lack a given card: assert every surviving layout lacks
  it, and the graded best card changes between the toggle off and on. Use
  `expert_inner_ratio = 1.0` and a large `N` so the inner mean is exact
  enough for a sharp assertion; run it in both strict and default mode —
  the survivors must agree on this position.
- **The diamond-ruff shape** from the user's example as a full-deal fixture:
  after East fails to return a diamond, declarer graded with the toggle on
  should see East's remaining-diamond frequency collapse.
- **Fast-accept never rejects**: on random positions, every layout the
  default mode rejects is also rejected by strict mode (subset property).
- **Memo correctness**: a repeated key is served from the memo (count
  `judged`), and equals a fresh evaluation with the same seed.
- **Sequential stopping is sound**: verdicts with early stopping agree with
  full-`M` verdicts except within the tolerance band, on a fixed seed.
- **Equivalence classes**: a seat holding only touching cards in the led
  suit is not a decision; touching cards separated by an *unplayed* card
  are two.
- **Budget exhaustion** returns the unfiltered grade with the marker.
- **All three frames**: a defender `o` and a declarer `o`, each judged
  correctly by the inner solve, and the trace's declarer-tricks sign read
  correctly for both sides.
- **Tolerance**: on a constructed position with a known single-dummy gap,
  a gap under `tol₀` is never rejected at any `M`; a gap of 1.0 trick is
  rejected at `M = 8`; a gap of 0.3 is kept at `M = 15` and rejected at
  `M = 50` (the scaling table); and a 50-50 guess (two cards with equal
  means, opposite on every deal) is always consistent — the test that the
  per-card-mean definition, not a per-deal cost, is what is implemented.
- **Both null hypotheses**: over many seeds on a position where the actual
  card is exactly tied with the best alternative, the false-rejection rate
  at `M = 15` is under 2%.
- API: the settings round-trip, are ignored when the toggle is off, change
  the cache key when on, and `decisions` chunks return exactly those indices.

## Decisions (user, 2026-09-01)

1. **Fast-accept by default, strict as a toggle.** The fast-accept is the
   difference between ~2 min and ~10+ min per seat on prod; its only cost is
   leniency toward plays that happened to work on the layout. `expert_strict`
   exists precisely for the case that matters there — a play that succeeded
   on the actual hand but was wrong single-dummy (the anti-percentage drop
   that found the queen) — and searches every opponent decision.
2. **Hard rejection.** A layout is in or out; the response counts survivors.
   Soft weighting (`Π_j sigmoid((tol − gap_j)/0.1)`, `_solve` taking weights,
   an effective sample size in place of the count) is a possible later
   refinement on the same data, not part of v1.3.
3. **Tolerance scales with the sample.** `tol₀ = 0.10` plus `z·σ̂/√M` with
   `z = 2`, giving the table in *Tolerance and noise* — 0.41 tricks at the
   default (now `N = 60` after the 2× scaling of every deal count: 0.32),
   0.27 at `N = 100`. Step 1 of the implementation
   measures σ̂ on the example hands; if it is far from 0.6 the table is
   recomputed, the rule stands. The user's note: the table's exact values are
   provisional and expected to move with experimentation — the scaling rule
   is the decision, not the numbers.
