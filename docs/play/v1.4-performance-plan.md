# Play v1.4 — faster strict computation, same accuracy

One of the two focus areas folded into v1.4 (see `ROADMAP.md`). Goal: cut the
expert-strict cost per decision by an order of magnitude **locally**, gated on
the board-7 benchmarks (`docs/play/bench/`, `scripts/bench_play.py`).

## Measured facts (2026-09-03, dev laptop, 16 cpus)

Benchmark (`board7-*-strict100`): strict ≈ 60–105 s/graded decision, worst
586 s; plain ≈ 0.17 s. Memo hit rate 80–90 % across a seat.

cProfile of one cold strict decision (o7, N's trick-2 lead, 40 deals,
111 fresh judgements, 30.2 s wall):

| where | time | share |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `SolveAllBoardsBin` (DDS) | 29.0 s in **401 calls** | 96 % |
| layout sampling (`ExactDealSampler`) | 0.5 s | 1.7 % |
| deal building + everything else | ~0.7 s | 2.3 % |

The 401 calls solved 2 040 boards — **~5 boards per call**, because the inner
judgement loop solves sequentially in `INNER_BATCH = 5` steps to stop early
when the paired-difference CI settles. DDS parallelises *across boards within
one call*: a 5-board call on 4 threads costs ~0.4 board-times per board; a
200-board call on 16 threads costs ~0.06. So the machine is idle ~85 % of the
time we are inside DDS. Python overhead is noise; **batch geometry is the
whole game.**

## Steps, in order of value per effort

### 0. Instrument, so every later step is measurable

Counters in `dds_runtime` (calls, boards, wall time inside DDS, per batch
function), surfaced per request and printed by `bench_play.py`. ~30 lines.
Everything below quotes these numbers before/after.

### 1. Use the cores locally (no behaviour change at all)

`BRIDGE_DDS_THREADS=16` for local runs (dev docs + skill note, *not* the
prod compose files — the VPS has 2 cores and concurrent users). DDS results
are exact and thread-count-independent: bit-identical output, zero risk.
Helps the big calls (outer `_solve` at 100 boards, traces at 20) immediately;
helps the inner loop only after step 2 widens its batches.

### 2. Widen the inner batch schedule

Replace the fixed 5-step with a geometric schedule sized to the thread count:
first batch `min(inner_cap, max(8, DDS_THREADS))`, then ×2 per round up to
`inner_cap` (M = 50 at N = 100 → at most 3 rounds instead of 10). Early
stopping keeps working; the waste bound is one final batch on judgements that
would have stopped mid-schedule — cheap next to the per-call loss today.

**Accuracy note (the one real decision):** the sequential test consumes RNG
draws in a different order, so borderline verdicts can flip and the board-7
grades may shift on genuinely marginal judgements. The estimator is unchanged
and unbiased; what changes is which sample a marginal verdict saw. Gate
re-defined accordingly (below).

### 3. Aggregate solves across judgements

Within one graded decision, suspects are judged one at a time, each firing
its own small solves. Queue instead: collect the *first* batch of inner deals
for every pending suspect of the round, solve them in one ≤200-board
`solve_all`, distribute results, iterate on the survivors. Turns hundreds of
5-board calls into a handful of near-full calls; combined with step 2 this is
the path to the ~6× in-process ceiling. Medium refactor of
`expert_layouts`/`judge` (the memo and verdict logic are untouched — only
*when* solves happen moves).

### 4. Worker processes (beyond the in-process ceiling)

libdds is process-global and non-reentrant; threads cannot help across calls.
W worker processes (each its own libdds + `BRIDGE_DDS_THREADS = cores/W`),
partitioning **outer layouts** per graded decision. Determinism survives:
verdicts are already seeded per `(context, index, view, holding)` key, so a
layout's verdict is the same whichever worker computes it. The memo becomes
per-worker — with an 80–90 % hit rate that loss is real, so partition
layouts *round-robin by holding* where possible, and measure before deciding
on a shared-memory memo. Target a further ~2–3× on 16 cores. Prod (2 cores)
keeps 1 process; this is a local/dev lever first.

### 5. Alternative solver spike (only after 1–4)

Researched options:

- **macroxue/bridge-solver** (C++, Apache-2.0/MIT): claims 1.5× DDS 2.9
  single-threaded (2026), scales ~6.4× on 16 cores via *processes*. CLI only
  — no library API; integration = subprocess protocol or extracting the core.
- **bcalc** (Piotr Beling): ~2× DDS claimed by its author, but closed source
  and slower than macroxue in the same comparison.
- **ddss** (Rust fork of DDS 2.9): Apache-2.0, no Python bindings,
  non-reentrant like DDS — nothing structural gained.

Since 96 % of time is DDS, a 1.5× solver multiplies with everything above —
but it is the highest-integration-cost lever and the accuracy gate would need
a full cross-check (two solvers disagreeing on any board is a stop-the-line
event). Spike only if steps 1–4 leave us short of the target.

## The accuracy gate

**Decided 2026-09-03:** RNG draw-order changes are acceptable; the gate is
*within noise of the right result*, not bit-exactness. Local and VPS may run
different solver configs going forward; this focus area optimises against
**local profiling** first.

`bench_play.py` strict-100 on both rooms of `docs/play/board7.lin`:

- **Step 1:** byte-identical JSON (thread count cannot change DDS results).
- **Steps 2–4:** per-decision **status must match** the committed baseline
  and `diff`/`imp_diff` within ±0.05 tricks / ±0.5 IMPs; any status change
  must be shown to be a genuinely borderline judgement (gap within the
  threshold band), documented in the bench notes, and the new baseline
  committed alongside the change.
- Timing columns are the success metric: target ≤ 10 s/decision strict-100
  in-process (steps 1–3), ≤ 5 s with workers (step 4), from ~96 s today.

## Results — steps 0–3 built (2026-09-03)

- **Step 0:** counters live in `dds_runtime` (`stats()` / `reset_stats()`,
  `GET /api/play/debug/dds`); `bench_play.py` records per-seat deltas;
  `scripts/compare_bench.py` is the gate check.
- **Step 1:** confirmed no-op on its own — 16 threads at 5-board calls gained
  nothing (31.0 s vs 30.0 s on the probe decision). The skill notes the local
  `BRIDGE_DDS_THREADS=16` + `127.0.0.1` rules.
- **Step 2+3:** geometric inner schedule (`INNER_FIRST = max(5, DDS_THREADS)`,
  ×2 per round) + wavefront aggregation. **First attempt was a lesson:**
  batching *every* memo-missed suspect up front tripled fresh judgements
  (o7 W: 5 747 → 19 225) and ran 0.7× — the probe couldn't see it because a
  single cold decision judges everything anyway. The shipped shape judges the
  k-th suspect of all still-alive layouts per wave, so nothing past a
  layout's first rejection is judged and the fresh count matches sequential.
- **Measured:** probe decision 30.0 → 12.7 s (16 threads), boards/call
  5.1 → ~95–140, ms/board 14.4 → 5.0. Whole-board strict-100: o7
  2 378 → 1 059 s (2.2×), c7 3 829 → 1 824 s (2.1×) — ~30 s/decision from
  ~96. Worst single decision remains ~500 s (one intrinsically hard East
  judgement per room; it moves with the draw order).
- **Gate:** c7 identical grades. o7 re-baselined with two documented
  borderline flips (N T5 ♣T −0.09→−0.14 across the 0.1 optimal cutoff;
  E T4 ♥J −0.27→−0.33 across the −0.3 good cutoff) and one magnitude drift
  on the headline blunder (T7 ♠3 −5.94→−4.53 IMPs, verdict unchanged —
  big-swing decisions carry proportionally more per-sample IMP variance,
  hence the gate's 25% relative IMP tolerance).
- **Then open:** step 4 (worker processes) and step 5 (solver spike). Step 4
  was measured and closed the next day — see the results section below.
  Prod (2-core) gets the aggregation win (~1.5×) with unchanged defaults.

### Finding: cost tracks play quality (o7 1 059 s vs c7 1 824 s)

Refuting a blunder is cheap; verifying clean play is expensive. Grading a
defender samples hypothetical declarer hands — in o7 the recorded declarer
line contains real errors, so ~99 % of layouts die on their first suspect
(usually a memo hit) and past the budget the filter returns unfiltered pools:
o7's defenders graded in ~120 s at **1 % acceptance**. c7's near-expert line
accepts 16–67 %, and accepting a layout means judging *every* suspect on it,
with borderline judgements running to the inner cap — that is where the time
goes (worst single decision ~500 s, c7 East).

Two consequences for the accuracy focus area: (1) at 1 % acceptance the
zero-survivor fallback means o7's defender strict grades are largely
*unfiltered* — by design and marked (`inference: none`), but worth surfacing
better in the UI; (2) design question: after a play the user acknowledges as
an error, should that play be exempted from the consistency requirement so
the filter stays alive for the rest of the hand?

## Results — step 4 refuted, the judgement floor reached (2026-09-04)

### Step 4 (worker processes): measured, not worth building

The question was whether libdds's in-process ceiling leaves cores idle. A
microbenchmark solved the same 128 sampled boards (c7, trick-one position,
the expensive kind) under every split of the 16 logical / 8 physical cores:

| configuration | ms/board |
| --- | --- |
| 1 process × 1 thread | 21.4 |
| 1 process × 4 threads | 6.8 |
| 1 process × 8 threads | 4.5 |
| **1 process × 16 threads** | **3.9** |
| 2 processes × 8 threads | 4.5 |
| 4 processes × 4 threads | 4.9 |
| 8 processes × 2 threads | 6.0 |
| 16 processes × 1 thread | 8.2 |

One process with 16 threads already gets 5.5× out of 8 physical cores; every
multi-process split is equal or slower (later positions show the same shape
at lower absolute cost). `SolveAllBoards` parallelises across boards well
enough once a call carries ≥ 100 boards — which step 3's aggregation
guaranteed — so there is no idle CPU for worker processes to claim. Step 4 is
closed without code, and the remaining lever is **solving fewer boards**.

### What was built instead

Three changes, all with **bit-identical grades** (not just within the gate):

1. **Memo-first rejection + priority ordering of suspects**
   (`expert._resolve`). A layout is consistent iff *every* suspect's verdict
   is, and each verdict is a pure function of its memo key (seeded per key),
   so the accept/reject answer cannot depend on which suspects are judged or
   in what order — only the cost can. The old loop judged suspects
   latest-first, depth by depth. The new one consults the memo for all of a
   layout's suspects first (a remembered rejection settles it for free), then
   judges one pending suspect per wave, choosing per layout the one with the
   best *expected rejection probability per second* — both learnt as the
   waves run (`VerdictMemo.rejections` / `.costs`, keyed on the play without
   the holding). On the hardest benchmark decision (c7 East T7) the
   rejections come almost entirely from declarer's trick-5 club (571 of 752
   holdings rejected, 5 ms a judgement) while the latest-first order paid for
   trick-8/9 judgements on every layout first and then the expensive trick-2
   ones on the survivors.
2. **Direct DDS board construction** (`grader._build_deal` writes endplay's
   `deal` struct from the layout and the position: remaining-card bitmasks,
   the trick on the table, its leader) instead of parsing PBN and replaying
   the history card by card, and **direct result reads** (`grader._collect`
   walks DDS's `futureTricks` arrays instead of endplay's Card iterator).
   Pinned against the reference paths on random positions in
   `tests/test_play_fastpath.py`, including a DDS-output equality check.
3. **`sampling.hand_list_to_str`** via per-suit bitmasks and a 8 192-entry
   mask→string table (called once per sampled hand; it was 2.5% of a strict
   decision on its own).

The cProfile of a cold strict decision before these (c7 East T2, 84 s):
DDS 85%, deal building 5%, layout sampling 5%, result collection 4%.

### Measured

Probes (one cold strict decision in-process, 100 deals, 16 threads):

| decision | before | after (same verdicts) | fresh judgements |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| c7 East T2 ♣Q | 84.4 s (DDS 71.6) | 73.6 s (DDS 71.7) | 687 → 687 |
| c7 East T7 ♥7 | 93.7 s | 74.0 s | 2 105 → 1 386 |

The first is the pure Python-overhead win (wall − DDS fell from 12.8 s to
1.9 s; every rejection there comes from one play, judged first either way).
The second is the ordering win: rejections come from declarer's trick-5 club
(571 of 752 holdings, 5 ms a judgement) and the priority order reaches it
before the trick-2/3 judgements at 90 ms each.

Board-7 strict-100 through the API, both rooms sequential, cold process,
16 threads (`docs/play/bench/`, `scripts/compare_bench.py`):

| run | o7 total | o7 N / E / W | c7 total | c7 S / E / W |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| before (sequential rerun of 0e0a431) | 1 077 s | 804 / 124 / 149 | 1 569 s | 707 / 454 / 409 |
| after, old memo key (like-for-like) | 892 s (1.21×) | 675 / 120 / 97 | 1 214 s (1.29×) | 629 / 332 / 253 |
| after, fixed memo key | 1 177 s | 680 / 214 / 282 | 1 312 s (1.20×) | 533 / 460 / 319 |
| **+ opening lead exempt (committed)** | **707 s (1.52×)** | 158 / 246 / 302 | **986 s (1.59×)** | 187 / 474 / 325 |

Like for like, every grade is identical in c7 and the o7 declarer; the
speed-up is 1.2–1.3× overall and up to 1.6× on defender seats. With the
memo-key fix (next section) the o7 *defenders* now do the judgements the old
key was skipping — East examines 10 672 layouts and keeps 991 where before
it kept 200 of 16 344 and graded mostly unfiltered — so o7's total ends
above the like-for-like figure; c7, whose dummy plays happened to land the
same verdict on every layout, keeps its 1.2×. Per graded decision, strict
now costs ~34 s (declarer) and ~21–42 s (defenders) on this machine.

**Opening lead exempt (ruled 2026-09-04, see the section below).** Declarer
seats 680 → 158 s (4.3×) and 533 → 187 s (2.8×) with every status identical
and every number inside the gate (o7 N's IMP loss 5.31 → 5.81, c7 S's 4.23 →
4.01, both within the 25 % band — the 5 % of layouts the lead used to reject
are back in the sample); defender seats unchanged by construction. A strict
declarer decision is now ~8–9 s on this machine, from ~34 s.

**Where the time is.** After these changes DDS is 97 % of a strict decision
at ~3.5 ms/board on 16 threads, one call carries ~120 boards, and the fresh
judgement count sits at its floor: an accepted layout needs *every* suspect
judged, an opponent's hand differs on every layout, so a defender decision at
trick 7 costs ~100 accepted × ~10 declarer decisions × ~30 boards ≈ 30 000
boards ≈ 100 s whatever the order. The 10 s/decision target is therefore out
of reach for strict mode without changing what a verdict means (fewer inner
boards, a looser test) or a faster solver — both outside this focus area's
gate. Non-strict (the default) profits from the same changes plus the trace
batch: 20 → 200 plays per `analyse_plays` call, measured identical and 1.5×
faster (20.2 → 13.3 ms/layout).

**Thread count and the pins.** Since step 2 the inner schedule starts at
`max(5, DDS threads)`, so expert verdicts are thread-dependent (plain grades
are not): the P-1.3-2 pin (board 17, West, 60 deals) reproduces exactly at
`BRIDGE_DDS_THREADS=4` — 900 of 1 406, 13/2/0, 0.48 tricks, 0.96 IMPs — and
reads 0.52 / 1.00 at 16 threads with the same counts. Replay pins at 4.

### Finding: a play from dummy was memoised on dummy's holding (v1.3 bug, fixed)

The ordering change exposed it. The like-for-like gate on the open room
passed for declarer but East's and West's later decisions changed: where the
baseline had examined 2 000 layouts and kept none (grades marked
`inference: none`, i.e. unfiltered), the reordered run kept 100 of 520.
Diffing the two verdict memos for East's trick-3 decision left exactly one
key with a different verdict — declarer's ♦A from dummy at trick 2, judged
through declarer's eyes — `shown-worse` in one run, `clear` in the other.

The key was `(context, index, view, holding, card)` with `holding` = the
cards of the seat that *played* — for a play from dummy, dummy's. But dummy is
public and identical on every sampled layout; what the verdict depends on,
and what varies between layouts, is **declarer's** hand behind it (hidden from
the grading defender). So every layout shared one key, and one arbitrary
layout's verdict — whichever was judged first — decided them all: the old
order judged a layout on which the ♦A looked wrong and rejected all 2 000; the
new order judged one on which it looked right and accepted them. Neither was
an inference about the layouts.

Fix: `holding` is the **judging** seat's remaining cards (declarer's for a
play from dummy), which is everything the verdict depends on; the seeded inner
sample keys on the same tuple. Effects: (1) defenders' expert grades are now
genuinely filtered where they were silently unfiltered (or filtered by a coin
toss) — the bench's o7 defender rows show it; (2) dummy plays now cost one
fresh judgement per distinct declarer hand instead of one per decision, so
defender decisions got slower again where the earlier finding ("cost tracks
play quality") had put them at 1 % acceptance for the wrong reason;
(3) declarer's own grades are untouched (the defenders' plays were keyed
correctly), and so is the non-strict P-1.3-2 pin (declarer's seat). The
regression test is `test_a_play_from_dummy_is_keyed_on_declarers_holding`.
The plain single-dummy grades were never affected — only expert mode judges
opponents' plays.

### Ruling: the opening lead is not judged by the filter (2026-09-04)

Measured on two declarer decisions (c7 S T3, o7 N T3) with the per-play
cost/rejection counters: the lead — judged from the leader's view with dummy
unseen, so a full-deal solve with three hidden hands on every inner deal —
cost 0.27 s per layout against 0.04–0.09 s for any later play, was re-judged
on every fresh layout of every declarer decision, and rejected 5 of 111 and
6 of 108 layouts. The weakest inference at the highest price. It also sat
oddly beside the standing ruling that leads are the Opening Lead Simulator's
problem and are not graded here. `opponent_decisions` now skips index 0; the
first judged opponent card is trick 1, card 2. The user chose this over the
other candidate (carrying the consistent pool forward between decisions,
~1.5–2× on every seat, same distribution but not the same draw), which
remains open.

### Carrying the consistent pool forward (2026-09-04)

The other structural waste: every decision of a seat drew a fresh pool and
judged every earlier opponent play on it again, so the fresh-judgement count
grew with the index (c7 S: 105, 208, 354 … 1 145 per decision; b14 W: 93,
193, 430, 879, 998, 1 094, 1 232, 1 349, 1 441). `expert_layouts` now starts
from the layouts the same seat's previous decision accepted
(`VerdictMemo.pool_put/pool_before`, keyed like the verdicts plus the view
and the user's constraints): each is kept if the seats the view can see are
its real hands (dummy joins at trick one) and the play since is legal on it
(`_carry_forward` replays it — the hidden seats hold what they played and
followed suit), its earlier verdicts are memo hits, only the plays since get
judged, and fresh layouts are drawn for the deficit as before.

Why the sample is still right: a uniform draw from the layouts consistent
with the play to j, filtered by "consistent with the cards played since", is
a uniform draw from the layouts consistent with the play to j′; the expert
predicate is deterministic per layout, so carried and fresh layouts are
rejection samples from the same distribution. What changes is the draw —
the gate's ruling — and one wrinkle: a decision's sample now depends on
whether the process graded an earlier decision of that seat first (the UI
and the whole-table flow always do; an out-of-order request just misses the
pool and draws everything itself). Each grade's own sample is unbiased;
successive grades of a seat share layouts, which affects nothing reported.

Measured (strict-100, 16 threads, cold API, decisions in index order):

| room | lead exempt | + carry-forward | declarer seat | defender seats |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| o7 | 707 s | 574 s (1.23×) | 158 → 110 s | 246 → 195, 302 → 269 s |
| c7 | 986 s | 853 s (1.16×) | 187 → 122 s | 474 → 396, 325 → 336 s |
| b14 (2♥x) | 459 s | 440 s (1.04×) | 194 → 160 s | 135 → 139, 130 → 142 s |

Declarer seats carry about half their pool from one decision to the next
(the two hidden hands play one card between them) and gain 1.2–1.5×;
defender seats carry almost nothing (declarer *and* partner each play two
or three cards between a defender's decisions, so few layouts hold them
all) and gain nothing. Statuses identical on both board-7 rooms; on b14 two
of West's calls moved optimal → good (T5 ♦3 −0.22 → −0.61 IMPs, T7 ♦6
−0.47 → −1.13), both sitting on the 0.5-IMP demotion line.

**Noise finding, worth more than the speed-up.** To tell a draw change
from a bias, the two most-moved decisions were re-run cold at seeds 0–4
(no pools): c7 South's T9 ♦6 — the room's headline error — reads −0.43,
−0.07, −0.03, −0.03, −0.33 tricks (−3.8 to −1.4 IMPs), flipping between
*suboptimal* and *good* on the seed alone; b14 West's T7 ♦6 reads −0.04 to
−0.06 tricks (−0.44 to −0.58 IMPs) cold and −0.04 to −0.08 with a carried
pool at the same seeds, so the carried sample is unbiased and the bench's
−0.11 was a tail draw of a pool chained through nine earlier decisions.
At 100 deals, a big-swing decision's *status* is a coin toss near either
threshold; the gate's per-decision tolerances are tighter than the
estimator, and the accuracy focus area should treat sample size (or a
confidence band on the status) before anything else.

### Lazy inner sampling (2026-09-04)

With judgements at their floor, the defender profile (c7 E T7, cold, under
cProfile) read DDS 85 %, inner-sample generation 11 %, the rest 4 %. Each
judgement drew its whole inner sample up front — 50 layouts at 100 deals,
~63 draws for 50 because show-out constraints reject some — while about
half the judgements settle on their first 16-board wave. `_prep` now hands
the judgement a `LayoutStream` (`sampling.LayoutStream`, the same seeded
draw order taken in chunks; `generate_layouts` is the stream taken in one
go) and `_judge_batch` / `_judge_fresh` draw each wave as they reach it.
Bit-identical: the first k layouts of a seeded stream are the same however
many are drawn after them (`test_layout_stream_taken_in_chunks_equals_the_
eager_sample`; the c7 E T7 probe reproduces its decision and its 69 110
boards exactly).

Measured under the profiler on that decision: draws 115 920 → 92 245,
sampling 13.9 → 8.8 s, non-DDS time 18.6 → 13.0 s (−30 %), i.e. ~5 % of
the decision. The bench timings below carry a caveat: after five hours at
full load the laptop's DDS throughput had drifted (the same 69 110 boards
took 86 s early in the evening and 102 s late), so the per-seat *non-DDS*
time — wall minus the DDS counters the bench records — is the metric here,
not the wall clock.

Bench (lazy vs the carry-forward run, every grade, option, sampled and
kept count identical on all nine seats):

| room | carry-forward | + lazy sampling | non-DDS per seat | DDS ms/board |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| o7 (back to back) | 568 s | 535 s | 18 / 18 / 22 → 16 / 16 / 19 s | −5 % |
| c7 | 853 s | 814 s | 14 / 19 / 24 → 13 / 16 / 19 s | unchanged |
| b14 | 440 s | 384 s | 24 / 16 / 12 → 21 / 14 / 10 s | −12 % (cooled) |

The DDS column is the machine, not the code (identical board counts). The
first o7 attempt ran while the laptop was throttled (684 s, DDS +15–25 %
per board) and was repeated back to back against the carry-forward commit
from a worktree, both runs cold: overhead −12–14 % per seat, wall 1.06×.
Where the DDS rate held (c7) the overhead fell 15–20 %, matching the
profiler. The committed bench files are the lazy run for all three boards.

Two facts the bench exposed while proving identity: (1) `judged` and
`memo_hits` differ by a few units between otherwise identical runs, because
`VerdictMemo.priority` divides by *measured* seconds, so which suspect a
layout judges first can differ — the accept/reject answer cannot, and no
grade moved; (2) the earlier assumption that DDS throughput is stable
across an evening is wrong on this laptop — compare runs by DDS counters
(boards, ms/board) as well as wall time, and re-run a baseline when a
comparison matters.

### Baseline check

The committed 2026-09-03 baselines were re-run sequentially from a worktree at
that commit, same machine, same 16 threads, before the comparison (their
timestamps suggested the two rooms had overlapped; they had, by about twenty
seconds). o7 reproduced (1 077 s vs 1 059 s); c7 came in at 1 569 s vs
1 824 s, the difference almost all in East's two slowest decisions (454 s vs
822 s for the seat) — run-to-run machine noise on the longest DDS stretches,
not a code difference (grades identical both times). The sequential rerun is
the "before" column above; the committed files were the looser number.

## What is next (as of 2026-09-04)

Strict mode is at the floor under the current definition of a verdict:
DDS is 97 % of a decision at ~3.5 ms/board (trick one) to ~1.3 ms (late) on
16 threads, every accepted layout needs every suspect judged, rejected
layouts cost ~1.1 judgements, and the inner sample is drawn only as far as
the sequential test reads it. In order:

1. **Accuracy first, and it is a sample-size problem** (user's KIV
   2026-09-04): at 100 deals a big-swing decision's status flips with the
   seed alone (c7 T9 ♦6: −0.03 to −0.43 tricks over five seeds). Options to
   design: more deals only where a status sits near a threshold, or a
   confidence band on the status. Cost is deals × suspects × inner cap,
   i.e. quadratic in the deal count, so spend deals selectively. Measured
   and designed 2026-09-08: `v1.4-sample-size-plan.md`.
2. **Guess-aware grading** (strategy fusion), the second v1.4 accuracy item,
   after 1.
3. **Timing beyond the floor** needs one of two doors: a faster double-dummy
   core (step 5, ≤ 1.5×, big integration, exactness cross-check) or a
   cheaper verdict (fewer inner boards, a different test) — the latter is
   accuracy territory and belongs with 1.
4. Small follow-ups: `VerdictMemo.costs` counts per wave, not per
   judgement (ordering quality only); `inference: none` could be surfaced
   better in the UI; the whole-table flow could reuse a seat's pools across
   the two defenders' requests only if their views shared a hidden hand —
   they do not, so nothing to do there.

Measured and closed, do not re-propose: worker processes; transposition-
table reuse across positions of one deal inside a `SolveAllBoards` call; a
5-first inner schedule on 16 threads; trace-guided per-layout ordering in
strict mode.

## Non-goals

- No prod deployment changes in this focus area (2-core box, concurrency via
  the query pattern, DDS_LOCK semantics all stay).
- No GPU work — no practical GPU double-dummy solver exists.
- The guess-aware grading focus area is planned separately.
